- Jnana yoga, means (deck, not yet checked) | "  - **Jnana yoga, means (deck, not yet checked) (checked: Guidebook ch.1 sec 1.5):** the path of discerning the real from the unreal, strictly non-dual (Advaita Vedanta) and contemplative. Its three stages are shravana (listening to the teaching), manana (reflection and reasoning) and nididhyasana (repeated meditation to make the conviction firm). The Guidebook says the student prepares through the sadhana chatustaya (the fourfold preparation) and studies Vedanta under a realised teacher. The standard fourfold list is viveka, vairagya, the group of six virtues (shama, dama, uparati, titiksha, shraddha, samadhana) and mumukshutva; this list is not spelt out in the Guidebook. The slide heading that puts manas, buddhi, chitta and ahamkara under the sadhana chatustaya is wrong (those are the antahkarana)."
- Bhakti yoga, means (deck, not yet checked) | "  - **Bhakti yoga, means (deck, not yet checked) (checked: Guidebook ch.1 sec 1.5; Bhagavata Purana 7.5.23 via web search):** the path of unconditional, intense love for God; the Narada Bhakti Sutra is a premier text on bhakti and prema; kirtan (devotional chanting) is described as perhaps the most important technique. The Bhagavad Gita (7.16) names four kinds of devotee: the distressed (arta), the inquisitive (jijnasu), the seeker of wealth (artharthi) and the wise (jnani). The Bhagavata Purana (7.5.23) lists nine forms of devotion: hearing, chanting, remembering, serving the feet, worship, prayer or salutation (vandana), service as a servant, friendship and self-surrender; the slide leaves out vandana and its examples differ from the usual list, so give the forms and leave out the exemplar names unless checked."
- Hatha yoga, in more detail (deck, not yet checked) | "  - **Hatha yoga, in more detail (deck, not yet checked) (checked: Guidebook ch.1 sec 1.5):** physically oriented; the Guidebook says it is sourced from Tantra, and that most hatha systems begin with the purification kriyas (shat kriyas) whereas Patanjali's eight limbs begin with yama and niyama. Texts named: Hatha Yoga Pradipika (Svatmarama), Gheranda Samhita, Goraksha Samhita (Gorakshanath), Shiva Samhita, Yoga Ratnavali, Yoga Tarangini and others. The Pradipika explains hatha as ha (sun) and tha (moon), the union of the pingala (sun) and ida (moon) channels; kundalini then rises through the sushumna, crosses the chakras and unites at the sahasrara. Hatha is wrongly read as stubbornness. The aim is to control body and mind as a base for spiritual practice. The contrast with yama and niyama is about where practice begins, not a rejection of them."
